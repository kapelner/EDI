# Prevalence of Experimental Response Types Across Fields

Research date: September 16, 2026.

## Executive summary

Two findings dominate this review.

**First, the measured mix of response types depends almost entirely on the sampling frame, not on the state of the world.** Reviews restricted to the top general medical journals report binary and time-to-event outcomes at 30–47% and ~30% respectively, because those journals over-sample large, event-driven mortality trials. Reviews drawn PubMed-wide, or from publicly funded, cluster, paediatric, and specialty trials — where the overwhelming majority of the world's randomized experiments actually live — invert that picture, with continuous outcomes at 48–70%. Any single "X% of trials use response type Y" claim is really a claim about a journal list.

The same effect holds across fields, and it is larger still. Commercial web A/B testing is **70–90% binary conversions**; large UK and US education RCTs are effectively **100% continuous** test scores; alcohol trials are led by a **bounded proportion** at 66.1%; political-science survey experiments are dominated by ordinal rating scales. There is no field-independent answer, and no registry anywhere classifies primary outcomes by statistical type.

**Second, the six response types EDI implements today cover the overwhelming majority of scalar primary outcomes, and the one clearly missing scalar type is very rare.** `nominal` — the type most often proposed as EDI's gap — appears in the only quantitative estimate located anywhere at **0 of 7,132 outcomes (2000) and 11 of 8,299 outcomes, or 0.1% (2006)**. That independently corroborates the "defer indefinitely" recommendation already recorded in `../new_feature_plans/nominal_response_type_report.md`.

The more consequential gaps are not new scalar types but **structural features layered on top of existing types**: roughly **79%** of top-journal RCTs collect outcomes repeatedly, **12–29%** declare multiple primary outcomes, and **70–78%** of survival trials in susceptible populations face competing risks. These correspond to EDI's planned `longitudinal`, `multivariate`, and competing-risks work rather than to any new scale of measurement.

A working planning estimate for the share of randomized-experiment primary outcomes by measurement scale is **~40% continuous, ~35% binary, ~7% proportion, ~7% count, ~5% time-to-event, ~5% ordinal, ~1% nominal** (Table A). These are judgment-based syntheses across heterogeneous sampling frames, not a measured global distribution, and the ranges in the tables matter more than the point values. Weighting by raw experiment volume rather than by research literature would push binary well above 50%, since online experimentation dwarfs every other field by count.

One practical finding deserves flagging up front: **`proportion` is EDI's most under-appreciated implemented type.** It is nearly invisible in the clinical audit literature — one review offers a "percentage" column, at 2 of 100 — because taxonomies fold bounded fractions into "continuous". Yet it is the most common endpoint type in alcohol trials, a core metric class in large-scale online experimentation (ratio metrics, 20–35%), and the natural form of cognitive accuracy data and laboratory-economics games.

**A second sweep, prompted by follow-up questions, covers sample size, covariate counts, sequential design, and randomization method (Sections 3.5, 4.5, 5.4, 6.8, and Tables 8.8–8.10).** An early draft of that sweep conflated two different questions, and the report now keeps them explicitly separate: **interim monitoring** (when accumulated outcome data is analyzed for a stopping decision) versus **sequential allocation** (EDI's own `fixed`/`one-by-one`/`many-by-many` question — when and how the treatment-*assignment* mechanism itself runs). Three findings are worth stating up front. Sample size spans roughly **five orders of magnitude** across fields — from ~10 per condition in cognitive psychology to several million per online experiment — which is the single largest source of variation found anywhere in this report. Wherever a covariate-adjustment default exists, it converges on exactly **one covariate** (the pre-period or baseline value of the outcome itself), independently in clinical trials, online experimentation, and education. On interim monitoring, scheduled-batch cadences dominate wherever monitoring happens at all — zero of 441 sequential clinical trials across three independent reviews used continuous, subject-at-a-time monitoring, and major online platforms that expose a mathematically continuous statistical guarantee still operate it on a batched cadence because their underlying data pipelines are themselves batched. On allocation, `fixed` — a schedule generated before any subject enrolls — is the dominant real-world mechanism in both clinical trials and online experimentation, with genuinely adaptive `one-by-one` allocation (minimization, response-adaptive randomization, bandits) a real but consistently small minority everywhere measured.

## Scope and approach

This report summarizes a targeted web-based review of methodological audits, outcome-reporting reviews, registry analyses, and commercial experimentation benchmarks. It is not a systematic review with a preregistered search strategy, and no new registry extraction was performed. Every figure cited below was checked against the fetched source; figures derived by arithmetic are marked as derived and shown.

Two denominators recur and must not be conflated:

- **Per trial/experiment** — what share of experiments have a primary outcome of a given type.
- **Per outcome** — what share of all reported outcomes are of a given type. Trials report many outcomes (a mean of ~36.6 per trial in one audit), so outcome-level denominators shift results substantially toward whatever is cheap to measure repeatedly.
- **Per meta-analysis — excluded from this audit.** A meta-analysis is an aggregate of trials assembled by a reviewer, so counting meta-analyses counts reviewers' extraction choices rather than experimenters' design choices, and one trial may be counted many times or not at all.

**The inclusion rule is therefore: the unit of counting must be the randomized experiment or its outcomes, never a pooled analysis of them.** This excludes exactly two sources cited below, both discussed in Section 3.3 and neither used for any figure in Tables A or B. It does *not* exclude meta-analyses that report trial-level characteristics — when a paper pools 29 RCTs and tells us how those 29 trials measured their outcomes, the unit of counting is still the trial, and several such sources are used here.

**A structural caveat that shapes the whole report:** no registry-wide study (ClinicalTrials.gov/AACT, ChiCTR, CTRI) classifies primary outcomes by *statistical type* across all therapeutic areas. Outcome-type taxonomies are built by hand, one review at a time, and they disagree on categories — several fold bounded fractions into "continuous", and at least one labels a binary risk as a "proportion". This is a genuine hole in the literature rather than a gap in this search.

## 1. Two kinds of "response type"

EDI's `response_type` is a *scale of measurement*, and exactly one applies to a given scalar outcome. But most of EDI's planned future work is not a new scale; it is a *structural property* that co-occurs with any scale. A repeatedly-measured continuous outcome is both `continuous` and longitudinal.

So this report keeps two tables:

- **Table A (Sections 8.1–8.5)** — measurement scale of the primary outcome. Mutually exclusive; sums to ~100%. Given overall and as one subtable per field category.
- **Table B (Section 8.6)** — structural features. Overlapping; each row is an independent percentage and the column does not sum to anything.

Conflating the two is what produces impossible claims like "competing risks are 5% of outcomes." Competing risks are a property of a time-to-event outcome, not a rival to it.

Clinical trials are the only field with a real audit literature on outcome types, so they carry most of the quantitative weight in this report.

Its 2000 panel reads continuous 4,984/7,132 (69.9%), binary 1,961 (27.5%), ordinal 140 (2.0%), time-to-event 47 (0.6%), categorical 0.

### 3.2 Where each type concentrates

**Time-to-event** is a specialty phenomenon, not a general one. It reaches **63.4%** of 93 cardiothoracic surgery RCTs ([Robinson et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11708136/)) and dominates oncology, where 791 phase 3 trials split 35% overall survival and 63% alternative endpoints such as PFS ([Sherry et al. 2025, JAMA Oncol](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12127959/)) — those 791 trials enrolled 555,580 patients, a derived **≈702 per trial**. PFS as a primary endpoint went from **0% (0/167) in 1995–2004 to 42% (125/298) in 2010–2020** ([Del Paggio et al. 2021, JAMA Oncol](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7995135/)). In PubMed-wide samples the same type is 0.6–2%.

**Ordinal** outcomes are systematically undercounted, because a third to two-thirds of them are dichotomized before they reach any table. Among 42 stroke RCTs using an ordinal dependency scale as the primary outcome (32,432 participants, a derived **≈772 per trial**), **59.5% were analysed dichotomously and only 19.0% ordinally** ([Nunn et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4818820/)). A scoping review of 144 RCTs with ordinal outcomes in the four top journals found **33% dichotomized** and 44% using proportional odds ([Selman et al. 2024, Trials](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10998402/)); in 70 neurologic RCTs, **31% dichotomized** ([Long et al. 2025, Neurology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11793924/)). COVID-19 phase III trials are the high-water mark for ordinal endpoints, at **15.7%** of 153 primary endpoints in severe patients and **14.8%** of 481 in non-severe patients, driven by the WHO progression scale ([Sakamaki et al. 2021, Trials](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8575152/)).

**Count** outcomes cluster in respiratory, neurology, falls, and behavioral trials, and are usually modelled as continuous anyway. Across 54 brief alcohol intervention trials with 119 count outcome variables, **61.3% used linear models**, 31.1% used Poisson or negative binomial, and 5.0% used zero-inflated or hurdle models, despite **75.6% of outcomes being overdispersed** ([review, 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10841606/)). In multiple sclerosis, 22 phase 3/4 trials used annualised relapse rate as primary, with an appropriate count model for the analysis in 21 of 22 but for the sample size in only 7 of 22 ([Poncet-Megemont et al. 2022, Mult Scler](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/35450472)).

**Proportion** is the weakest-evidenced category in the entire literature. Only [Offorha et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8817506/) separates a "percentage" primary outcome at all, at **2 of 100**. Most taxonomies fold bounded fractions into "continuous", and [Rothwell et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6180439/) uses the word "proportion" to mean a binary risk. EDI's `proportion` type is therefore serving real outcomes that the audit literature has no column for — a measurement gap, not an absence.

**Nominal** is close to a void, and that is the finding. [Yu et al. 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2886040/) is the only quantitative estimate located: **0 of 7,132** outcomes in 2000 and **11 of 8,299 (0.1%)** in 2006. [Cro et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9396446/) has no nominal category in its taxonomy at all, and [Selman et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10998402/) reports no count of trials treating an ordinal outcome as unordered.

### 3.3 One excluded source and one real distortion

**Cochrane-derived figures are excluded from this audit** under the inclusion rule above, and the most widely quoted one is worth naming because it circulates as though it described trials. [Davey et al. 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3247075/) reports **66% dichotomous across 22,453 meta-analyses** in the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews. That is a count of meta-analyses entered in RevMan, not of trials: one trial can feed many meta-analyses, the outcomes extracted are chosen by the reviewer rather than the trialist, and RevMan's events-out-of-total entry format turns a time-to-event outcome into a 2×2 table, discarding timing and censoring.

[Salika et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8934481/) measures that last effect directly — **715 meta-analyses summarised all-cause mortality as binary** versus only **100** using observed-minus-expected/variance methods. It is retained here as evidence about *analysis practice*, namely that time-to-event information is routinely discarded, and not as evidence about outcome-type prevalence.

Counts of "continuous" outcomes are inflated in the other direction, because the category absorbs bounded scale scores and days-based composites. Patient-reported outcomes were the primary outcome in **37.6% of 303** HTA-journal RCTs ([Qian et al. 2021, BMJ Open](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8422492/)), and among 26 trials using ventilator-free days as primary, **96% could not accommodate the outcome's actual distribution** and only 8% assumed a zero-inflated form ([Renard Triché et al. 2023, Crit Care](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10394791/)). A meaningful share of what the tables call `continuous` is better served by `proportion`, `count`, or a semi-continuous model.

### 3.4 Geographic breadth

Trial volume is not concentrated where the audits are. Among 1,425 infertility RCTs published 2012–2023, the top three countries were **China (22%), Iran (20%), and Egypt (10%)**, with outcomes dominated by pregnancy and live birth (binary) and a hormone-level minority (continuous) ([Feng et al. 2025, Hum Reprod Open](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11842059/)). No registry-wide typed distribution exists for these literatures.

### 3.5 Sample size, covariate adjustment, and design timing in clinical trials

This subsection answers a broader ask than response type: how large clinical trials are, how many covariates their primary analyses adjust for, whether they are fixed or sequential, and — if sequential — whether they match EDI's `one-by-one` or `many-by-many` timing family. It draws on a dedicated follow-up sweep, separate from the response-type sweep behind Sections 3.1–3.4.

**Sample size.** The disease-specific corpora in Table 8.7 run 190–1,800 participants per trial because they are drawn from large, well-resourced specialties. The broad, unselected picture is much smaller. Three independent large-registry audits agree closely: median enrollment is **58** (IQR 27–161) across 40,970 trials registered 2007–2010 ([Califf et al. 2012, JAMA](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/1150093)), **60** (IQR 30–160) across 245,999 trials registered 2000–2019 ([Gresham et al. 2020, JAMA Netw Open](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2769830)), and **52–60** across two registries spanning 1999–2021 ([Barnett & Glasziou 2021, BMJ Open](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8719224/)). The registry-wide median has been *falling*, by 8–21% per five-year period depending on the metric — even as per-arm sizes within Cochrane-indexed trials specifically roughly doubled, from 33–45 (1975–79) to 74–92 (2010–14) ([Lamberink et al. 2018, J Clin Epidemiol](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/225730v1.full)). These are compatible: the registry-wide median is being pulled down by a growing share of small early-phase and pilot registrations, while trials that reach a Cochrane meta-analysis have grown.

Confirmatory trials are an order of magnitude larger: across **10,252 completed phase III trials**, median started N is **302** (IQR 118–616) and completed N is **228** (IQR 85–488) ([Chen et al. 2025, Br J Clin Pharmacol](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=EXT_ID:39979793&format=json&resultType=core)). Specialty varies even registry-wide: infectious disease trials run larger (median 125) than cardiovascular (73.5) or oncology (43) ([Goswami et al. 2013, PLoS One](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3797691/)).

**Covariate adjustment.** Roughly **73–84%** of RCTs in leading journals report some covariate-adjusted primary-outcome analysis (73.0% of 300 cluster RCTs, [Wright et al. 2015, J Clin Epidemiol](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4425474/); 84% of 298 individually-randomized RCTs, [Ciolino et al. 2019, BMC Med Res Methodol](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6610785/)), but the number of covariates used is consistently small. Among 241 trials in Ciolino's sample that adjusted for anything, **39% used exactly one covariate and 36% used exactly two — roughly three-quarters used at most two.** Full compliance, adjusting for *every* covariate used to stratify or minimize the randomization, is much rarer: **17.2%** ([Wright et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4425474/)) to **26%** ([Kahan & Morris 2012, BMJ](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3444136/)). Cluster RCTs adjust for somewhat more: a median of **3 individual-level covariates** (range 1–28) and **1 cluster-level covariate** (range 1–8) ([Wright et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4425474/)).

**A note on two different questions this report initially conflated.** EDI's `fixed`/`one-by-one`/`many-by-many` families describe **sequential allocation** — when and how the treatment-*assignment* mechanism runs, relative to when subjects enroll. That is a different question from **sequential testing / interim monitoring** — when accumulated *outcome* data is analyzed for a stopping decision (DSMBs, alpha-spending boundaries, group-sequential designs). A trial can use a completely fixed, pre-generated randomization list while still monitoring outcomes sequentially, or vice versa. The two paragraphs below cover monitoring; sequential allocation proper follows afterward.

#### Interim monitoring (sequential testing)

Fixed, single-final-look analysis of accumulated outcome data dominates. Only **33%** of 1,772 major-journal RCTs (2000–2005) reported any interim-monitoring mechanism at all — a Data Monitoring Committee, or an interim analysis without one — and of those, **78% ran to completion as planned** rather than stopping early ([Tharmanathan et al. 2008, BMC Med Res Methodol](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2279143/)). In unselected trial cohorts only about **5%** are ever discontinued specifically for benefit or futility, and **80% of those stops were not driven by a formal pre-specified stopping rule at all** ([cited within Walter et al. 2020, BMC Med Res Methodol](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6966801/)). A working estimate for major-journal trials is **fixed-analysis ≈65–75%, some interim monitoring ≈25–35%** — and this likely *overstates* prevalence across the full universe of RCTs, since a formal monitoring committee is a resource mostly large trials can afford. A stricter, all-registry measurement gives a much smaller figure for formally *adaptive* designs specifically: across 159,645 ClinicalTrials.gov registrations, explicitly identifiable adaptive designs rose from **0.11% to 0.38%** of all registrations between 2001–05 and 2012–13 — roughly a 3.5-fold increase, but still a small fraction of all trials, and flagged by the reviewing authors themselves as an undercount since registries under-report adaptive status ([Hatfield et al. 2016, Trials](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4799596/)). The two figures are not in tension: Tharmanathan's 33% counts *any* interim-monitoring mechanism, including ordinary group-sequential designs that trialists would not label "adaptive"; Hatfield's 0.11–0.38% counts only registrations explicitly self-identified as adaptive.

Among trials that do monitor sequentially, the cadence is overwhelmingly scheduled-batch rather than continuous — a finding that happens to have the same one-at-a-time-versus-batched shape as EDI's allocation families below, but is a distinct measurement (of monitoring cadence, not assignment timing). Across three independent systematic reviews of sequential and adaptive RCTs spanning 1980–2022 — **441 trials in total** — **zero used continuous, subject-at-a-time outcome monitoring; every one used a small number of pre-scheduled batch looks** at fixed information fractions ([Zhang & Saju 2023, n=56](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10483862/); [Stevely et al. 2015, n=68](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4631356/); [Ben-Eltriki et al. 2024, n=317](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11451232/)). The typical number of planned interim looks is small — one or two is most common (41% one look, 34% two, [Stevely et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4631356/)), skewing slightly higher in cardiovascular trials (42% used more than two, [Zhang & Saju 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10483862/)). Methodological guidance recommends more — "planning for between 4 and 8 interim analyses appears sensible" ([Todd et al. 2001, Br J Clin Pharmacol](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2014474/)) — but actual practice runs lower and varies by field: a cross-therapeutic-area EMA sample of adaptive trials used only a single look **67.7%** of the time ([Collignon et al. 2018, Trials](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6245528/)).

This is not an accident of modern practice; it has a documented history. Early sequential monitoring methods (Armitage, from the 1950s through 1975) really did analyse data after every patient — but this approach "did not gain general acceptance" because it was judged impractical, and Pocock's 1977 group-sequential framework became the standard specifically because it retains most of the efficiency of continuous monitoring while remaining operationally feasible ([Grayling, Wason & Mander 2017, arXiv:1710.03127](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1710.03127)). The gap has not closed since: a 2026 paper introducing a new continuous, per-subject monitoring method for event-rate surveillance describes itself as the field's "first practical implementation" of over twenty-year-old theory ([Kim & Han 2026, Statistics in Medicine](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12828109/)) — and even that implementation is for post-market drug and vaccine safety *surveillance* of observational data streams, not for randomized trial design, where group-sequential monitoring remains the norm ([Silva & Kulldorff 2015, Biometrics](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4888780/)).

#### Sequential allocation

This is the question EDI's `fixed` / `one-by-one` / `many-by-many` families actually answer: not when outcome data is analyzed, but when and how each subject's — or each batch's — treatment assignment is determined.

**Fixed allocation is the default, and most "randomization happening over time" is not actually sequential allocation.** The great majority of simple and permuted-block randomization in practice is implemented as a complete assignment schedule generated once, before enrollment begins; each arriving subject simply receives the next slot on that pre-generated list. Simple/complete randomization on its own is a small minority of named methods — **3%** of 152 phase III trials in top journals in 2010 ([Kahan, Rehal & Cro 2015, Trials](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4566301/)), **6%** of 330 trials published 2019 ([Bruce et al. 2022, BMC Med Res Methodol](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9727841/)) — but block and stratified-block randomization, which typically *also* run from a pre-generated schedule, make up the bulk of the rest: restricted randomization overall runs **60–90%** depending on overlap definitions (63% "restricted", Kahan et al.; 69% "stratified, all types", Bruce et al.), with **block-stratified randomization alone the single most common named method at 47%** ([Bruce et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9727841/)). A closely related extraction of the same 2019 cohort gives a slightly different breakdown — 385 individually-randomized trials, with stratified/stratified-block at 64%, purely random at 6%, and minimisation at 15% — matching the minimization and simple-randomization figures above exactly, with the small variation in the stratified share reflecting category-boundary choices between the two extractions rather than a real disagreement. **On the evidence available, `fixed` allocation therefore accounts for the substantial majority of clinical trials**, whether or not the resulting outcome data is later monitored sequentially. This is not a universal rule, though, and the two implementation models genuinely coexist in practice: a 2024 methods paper states plainly that unstratified randomization "can be pre-generated before the trial starts," while dynamic-balancing methods are "created dynamically, depending on the imbalances within the observed levels of the factors of the new patient" and mechanically "cannot" be pre-generated ([Sverdlov et al. 2024, BMC Med Res Methodol](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10900599/)). A 2010 multicenter-trials paper independently confirms both models are real: some systems use a "pre-generated center-specific randomization code list" where each arriving subject simply gets "the next available randomization code" (`fixed`), while others use "traditional central randomization" where "none of the treatment assignments are specified prior to the subject's enrollment" (genuinely live, `one-by-one`) — the paper proposes a named hybrid, "step-forward randomization," specifically to bridge the two ([Academic Emergency Medicine 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3052925/)). The correct summary is therefore: **plain simple and block randomization defaults to `fixed`; any stratified or dynamically-balanced system, including minimization, is genuinely `one-by-one` — and which one a given trial uses is implementation-dependent, not universal.**

**Minimization is a genuine one-by-one sequential allocation mechanism, and its own originators framed it that way from the start** — unlike a pre-generated block list, each new subject's assignment is computed live, using the accumulated allocation history at the moment they enroll, to keep covariate margins balanced. The foundational paper is literally titled "Sequential Treatment Assignment with Balancing for Prognostic Factors in the Controlled Clinical Trial" ([Pocock & Simon 1975, Biometrics](https://www.jstor.org/stable/2529712)) — the method was named as sequential by design, not reclassified as such by this report. It is real but secondary, typically **15–25%** in top journals (15%, Bruce et al.; 25% of 40 phase II GI-oncology trials, [Martin et al. 2024, Curr Oncol](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11202503/)) — but that share is itself a top-journal artifact: across the literature as a whole, all trial sizes and all journals, minimization use is **under 2%**, having roughly tripled over the preceding decade from a smaller base, and concentrated almost entirely in large, well-resourced trials rather than being common practice generally ([Taves 2010, Contemp Clin Trials](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/19913110)). The typical number of stratification factors is small: almost half of 185 stratified cluster RCTs used exactly one factor, and only 2% used four or more ([Borhan et al. 2020, Trials](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7672868/)); stratified-block trials use a median of one factor (range 0–3) versus three (range 1–7) for minimization trials, consistent with minimization's known capacity to balance more factors at once, live, than a pre-generated block list would ([Martin et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11202503/)).

**Rerandomization and matching-on-the-fly are, in principle, further sequential-allocation mechanisms, and the evidence here is more nuanced than an earlier pass through this report captured — one method's specific algorithm has no known real-trial adoption, but the broader design family it belongs to does.** EDI implements both a Mahalanobis-distance rerandomization family and a sequential matching-on-the-fly family (the `KK14`/`KK21` designs, named for their origin paper). Classical rerandomization (redraw the whole allocation upfront until a balance criterion is met) is itself typically a *fixed*-allocation method with an acceptance-sampling step, not a live per-subject procedure; a genuinely sequential, one-by-one rerandomization variant exists in the literature (Atkinson-style optimal sequential allocation) but as of a 2015 methods review had **zero known field implementations across clinical trials or economics**.

**KK14 specifically, and its direct statistical descendants, have no known real-trial adoption.** EDI's own `KK14` design — a one-by-one sequential allocation mechanism by construction, matching each new subject on-the-fly to a covariate-similar prior subject — traces to [Kapelner & Krieger 2014, Biometrics](https://academic.oup.com/biometrics/article/70/2/378/7424317), which validates the method through simulation. A direct descendant, sequential (re)matched randomization, was tested by applying it *retrospectively* to real baseline-covariate and enrollment-order data from the REACH type 2 diabetes trial (N=506) — but with synthetic outcomes generated under the Neyman potential-outcomes model, not the trial's actual results ([Chipman, Mayberry & Greevy 2023, Stat Med](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.13797)), which states plainly that "despite [covariate-adjusted randomization] advances, stratified randomization remains the most common" method. A citation check of the 26 papers citing Kapelner & Krieger (2014) found none reporting real-trial use. A 2026 proposal for smartwatch-guided matching is validated the same way, on archived COVID-19 data ([Shahmoon et al. 2026, Scientific Reports](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-52579-4)).

**But the broader family this design belongs to — live, sequential, covariate-based allocation — does have real, practice-relevant adoption, via methods related to but distinct from KK14 itself.** The REACH trial's *actual* allocation mechanism, as opposed to the retrospective reanalysis of its covariate data described above, was itself live: "clinical coordinators enrolled participants and sent baseline data to the statistician for weekly batch randomization," using optimal multivariate matching to balance covariates within each batch ([Chipman et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.13797); trial results in [Nelson et al. 2021, Diabetes Care](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7783936/)) — a real `many-by-many` covariate-matching allocation mechanism, in the lineage of Greevy, Lu, Silber & Rosenbaum's optimal multivariate matching, though distinct from KK14's pairwise on-the-fly matching. More strikingly, the ESCAPE stroke trial (N=316) — a major, practice-changing trial (Goyal et al. 2015, NEJM) — allocated its actual participants using Minimal Sufficient Balance, implemented "using a server-based system" where "randomization occurred dynamically in real time": genuine one-by-one, live, covariate-adaptive allocation, used to run a real, influential trial, not a simulation ([Sajobi et al. 2017, Trials](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5667454/)).

The one adoption estimate located for matched-pair designs specifically — roughly 1% of registered trials — comes from the AEA economics registry, not medicine, and should not be extrapolated across fields. **The accurate characterization is therefore narrower than "essentially unadopted": EDI's specific `KK14`/`KK21` algorithm and its direct statistical descendants have no known real-trial adoption, but the design family it belongs to — live sequential covariate-adaptive allocation, both `one-by-one` (ESCAPE) and `many-by-many` (REACH) — is validated in real, influential clinical practice, via methods that differ from KK14's specific pairwise-matching mechanism.**

**A minimization-family method is confirmed in live, present-day use, not just historically.** A direct ClinicalTrials.gov registry query found two currently-registered platform trials explicitly using "minimal sufficient balance" — a minimization-family method related to but distinct from classic Pocock-Simon minimization — self-described in one registration as "(minimization procedure)" (NCT06352632, an adaptive stroke platform trial; NCT07687056, a thrombolysis domain within the same platform).

**Response-adaptive randomization (RAR)** — allocation probability shifting with accumulating *outcome* data, not just covariates: play-the-winner, DBCD, Bayesian/Thompson-sampling designs — is real, growing, and well documented, but rare in absolute terms. The best available count identifies **65 planned or conducted RAR trials worldwide from 1985 through 2023** via a targeted literature-and-registry search, of which only 34 had completed with reported results ([Wilson et al. 2025, Statistical Methods in Medical Research](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12460923/)). No source gives a clean share of all RCTs, because none attempts that denominator, but every source consulted independently characterizes uptake as "disproportionately low" relative to RAR's large methodological literature — a controversial pair of 1980s ECMO trials is described as having chilled adoption "for the subsequent 20 years" ([Robertson et al. 2023, Statistical Science](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7614644/)). RAR trials concentrate in oncology (24.6% of the 65) and infectious disease/platform trials (10.8%), with named real examples including BATTLE, BATTLE-2, I-SPY2, and REMAP-CAP. A widely cited 2011 critique arguing RAR is statistically inferior to fixed-ratio randomization for information yield is credited across the literature as the central explanation for continued low uptake ([Korn & Freidlin 2011, J Clin Oncol](https://ascopubs.org/doi/10.1200/JCO.2010.31.1423) — citation confirmed across independent sources, full text not independently accessed).

**Stepped-wedge cluster trials look like `many-by-many` allocation but likely are not, on the evidence available.** They have a real and fast-growing prevalence literature: two independent systematic reviews found **25 trials by ~2010** and **60 by ~2014** (32 completed, 28 protocols) — a derived **2.4× increase** over roughly 4.5 years — with a median of 4 randomization steps per trial (IQR 2–6) ([Mdege et al. 2011, J Clin Epidemiol](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21411284/); [Martin et al. 2016, BMJ Open](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4746455/)). But a stepped-wedge design has *every* cluster eventually receive treatment — only the crossover *timing* is randomized — which is materially different from `many-by-many` allocation, where each batch's treatment-versus-control split is decided using that batch's own data as it arrives. The available evidence, while not fully settled by a directly quoted primary source, points toward the crossover-order schedule being drawn as a single event before the trial begins — closer to EDI's `fixed` family with staggered rollout timing, not genuine sequential batch allocation. This should be treated as the report's best current judgment on stepped-wedge's classification, not a fully verified fact.

**A second, independent method corroborates the matching-on-the-fly finding above.** Four separate ClinicalTrials.gov registry API queries — for "matching on the fly," "sequential matching" plus randomization, "matched-pair" plus sequential enrollment, and covariate-matching/adaptive phrasing — each returned zero results, reproducibly. This is registry-text-search evidence, a different method from the citation-graph check reported earlier in this subsection, and it reaches the same conclusion by an independent route.

**Reporting quality.** Non-reporting of the randomization method is persistent, and far worse outside curated top-journal samples: unstated in **35%** of BMJ/JAMA/Lancet/NEJM trials ([Kahan, Rehal & Cro 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4566301/)) and **57%** of stratified cluster RCTs failed to report the method used within strata ([Borhan et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7672868/)), but among 1,528 phase III protocols on ClinicalTrials.gov broadly, only **20.8%** described any randomization method at all ([Lai et al. 2021, Healthcare](https://digitalcommons.kansascity.edu/studentpub/180/)), and across 10,103 Cochrane-derived RCTs, **56.5%** did not describe their sequence-generation method ([Barcot et al. 2019, BMC Med Res Methodol](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6683577/)) — another instance of this report's recurring frame effect. **Cluster randomization's overall share of trials is genuinely unquantifiable**: ClinicalTrials.gov has no structured field distinguishing it, so the only available evidence is bibliometric publication-count growth — roughly one cluster RCT per year in the 1960s to over 120 per year by 2008 — not a share of all trials ([Moberg & Kramer 2015, JLL Bulletin](https://www.jameslindlibrary.org/articles/a-brief-history-of-the-cluster-randomized-trial-design/)).

## 4. Online controlled experiments (tech A/B testing)

This is the highest-volume experimental literature in the world by raw count, and it splits into two populations with materially different metric mixes.

### 4.1 Commercial web/CRO testing

Across **173,000 qualifying experiments from 1,200+ companies (2018–2026)**, **over 90% target five metrics: CTA clicks, pageviews, checkout, registration, and add-to-cart** — all of which are binary per-visitor conversions or event counts. Revenue-per-visitor and purchase rate together are used **over 20%** of the time ([Optimizely Field Notes](https://www.optimizely.com/field-notes/guides/173000-experiments), vendor-reported).

Independent corroboration from a peer-reviewed analysis of **2,766 experiments** run by 1,349 accounts: the platform's engagement click is "the default and most popular goal," and the conversion rate is defined as conversions divided by visitors — a per-visitor binary ([Berman & Van den Bulte 2022, Management Science](https://ron-berman.com/papers/fdr.pdf)). A vendor analysis of **28,304 experiments** describes the default as "the primary conversion rate goal" ([CXL/Convert 2019](https://cxl.com/blog/learning-analyzing-experiments/), vendor-reported).

### 4.2 Large-scale product experimentation

At Microsoft, LinkedIn, Booking, Spotify and Netflix the primary metric shifts away from simple conversion toward per-user means, counts and **ratio metrics**. Bing's OEC uses "sessions per user" (a count) and average revenue per user ([Kohavi et al. 2013, KDD](https://exp-platform.com/Documents/2013%20controlledExperimentsAtScale.pdf)); a JPE study of **1,505 Bing experiments** uses the session **success rate**, a proportion ([Azevedo et al. 2020](https://joseluismontielolea.com/azevedo-et-al-ab-supplementary-material.pdf)); and at LinkedIn, "**all of them are average metrics, such as revenue per member or clicks per impression**" ([Liu et al. 2019, arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1903.08762)).

Ratio metrics — where the analysis unit differs from the randomization unit — are near-universal at this scale: "**most user-randomized experiments run by ExP contain both user-level and page-level metrics**" ([Deng, Knoblich & Lu 2018, KDD](https://alexdeng.github.io/public/files/kdd2018-dm.pdf)). Delta-method variance estimation is a first-class feature in every major platform.

### 4.3 Three findings that matter for EDI

**Revenue metrics are always zero-inflated, and the industry hacks around it rather than modelling it.** A meta-analysis of **6,700 e-commerce experiments** defines revenue per visitor as including "visitors which do not purchase anything," notes that "revenue distributions are very discrete and multimodal," and **removes the top 0.1% of customers by revenue in each test** ([Browne & Swarbrick Jones 2017, Qubit](https://gwern.net/doc/economics/advertising/2017-browne.pdf), vendor-authored). Microsoft's own guidance recommends splitting Revenue per User into conversion rate × revenue per purchaser for power — an explicit hurdle decomposition ([Kohavi & Longbotham](https://exp-platform.com/Documents/2023-03-11EncyclopeiaMLDSABTestingFinal.pdf)). A recent review lists "capping (truncating)" and "**binarizing count metrics and revenue**" as standard practice ([Larsen et al. 2024, The American Statistician](https://arxiv.org/html/2212.11366v5)). This is EDI's planned semi-continuous type, with the workarounds already documented in industry practice.

**Every experiment at scale is simultaneously a multivariate problem.** Bing collects **an average of 1,409 metrics per experiment (SD 488)** across 21,220 experiments ([Kevic et al. 2017, ICSE-SEIP](https://exp-platform.com/Documents/2017-05%20ICSE2017_CharacterizingExP.pdf)); LinkedIn reports thousands per experiment; thirteen major organizations testing over 100,000 treatments a year analyse "hundreds or thousands of metrics" organized as OEC plus guardrails ([Gupta et al. 2019, SIGKDD Explorations](https://exp-platform.com/Documents/2019-FirstPracticalOnlineControlledExperimentsSummit_SIGKDDExplorations.pdf)). Even at the small end, **85%** of experiments track two or more goals (derived from the 15% that list engagement alone), with a mean of 4.18 goals and a maximum of 158 ([Berman & Van den Bulte](https://ron-berman.com/papers/fdr.pdf)).

**Ordinal and time-to-event responses are structurally absent from the tooling.** No major platform — Optimizely, GrowthBook, Statsig, Eppo — exposes an ordinal metric type or a true censored time-to-event metric; retention is implemented as a **binary indicator at a fixed horizon**. Survival analysis does appear as a real analysis mode: "Netflix also used survival analysis to take censoring of user data into account" ([Gupta et al. 2019](https://exp-platform.com/Documents/2019-FirstPracticalOnlineControlledExperimentsSummit_SIGKDDExplorations.pdf)). The absence of ordinal support is an evidence gap rather than a measured zero — no source in this sweep quantified NPS or star-rating metrics as primary.

### 4.4 Estimated metric-type mix

Estimates for both populations are tabulated in **Section 8.3**.

One corpus runs against the commercial grain and is worth noting because it is the largest public academic A/B dataset: the Upworthy archive's **32,487 experiments** use clicks over impressions — an aggregated binomial proportion — with a derived **~4.64 arms per test**, compared with the commercial sample's 64% two-arm majority (Table H2) ([Matias et al. 2021, Scientific Data](https://upworthy.natematias.com/about-the-archive.html)).

### 4.5 Sample size, covariate adjustment, and design timing in online experiments

This subsection draws on the same follow-up sweep as Sections 3.5, 5.4, and 6.8. One correction worth noting on its own: the CUPED paper is a **WSDM 2013** paper, not KDD as it is frequently cited — verified by reading the paper's own header.

**Sample size.** No public source gives an actual median, mean, or distribution of experiment size — every source gives either an eligibility floor or a single-company scale statement, and this looks like a genuine gap rather than a search failure. Bing's own published scale figures (100 million monthly US users, over 200 concurrent experiments, users rotated into 15+ experiments each) imply **several million users per experiment** ([Kohavi et al. 2013, KDD](https://exp-platform.com/Documents/2013%20controlledExperimentsAtScale.pdf); a derived cross-check from those same figures lands at ≈6.75 million, consistent with the paper's own qualitative statement). General guidance for smaller organizations is "at least thousands" of active users, with large sites running "hundreds of thousands to millions" ([Kohavi & Longbotham 2023](https://exp-platform.com/Documents/2023-03-11EncyclopeiaMLDSABTestingFinal.pdf)). Optimizely's 1,000-visitors-per-variant threshold, cited in Section 4.1, remains a floor across all 173,000 examined experiments — no median above it is published. The best-evidence range is roughly **10³ at the small-CRO end to 10⁶–10⁷ at the large-platform end**, several orders of magnitude above even the largest clinical or field-experiment figures in this report.

**Covariate adjustment (CUPED).** The real-world default is **one covariate** — the pre-experiment value of the same outcome metric — confirmed independently at Microsoft/Bing (the originating platform), Booking.com, and as LinkedIn's baseline implementation ([Deng, Xu, Kohavi & Walker 2013, WSDM](https://exp-platform.com/Documents/2013-02-CUPED-ImprovingSensitivityOfControlledExperiments.pdf); [Jin & Ba, Technometrics/arXiv:2110.13406](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.13406)). This is not an oversight: the original CUPED paper itself shows fast-diminishing returns beyond that single covariate, with additional covariates such as entry-day adding only "2 to 3% more" reduction. Multi-covariate and ML-based extensions exist and are growing — Yandex uses roughly 51 engineered features, LinkedIn's extension adds 7 member attributes, and DoorDash's CUPAC replaces many raw covariates with a single learned prediction ([Poyarkov et al. 2016, KDD](https://www.kdd.org/kdd2016/papers/files/adf0653-poyarkovA.pdf); Jin & Ba; DoorDash Engineering) — but they read as refinements layered on the single-covariate default rather than replacements for it. The variance-reduction magnitude is highly context-dependent, not a fixed "~50%": Microsoft's own later internal follow-up found most metrics on at least one product surface see an effective traffic multiplier under 1.05× (near-zero realized benefit), while LinkedIn's single-covariate baseline achieves 15.91% and Yandex's achieves 56.63%. **The frequently repeated "up to 50%" figure traces to exactly one 2013 Microsoft claim about three flagship examples and should not be treated as typical.** CUPED or an equivalent is now near-standard practice among large-scale experimentation programs — independently confirmed at Microsoft, Netflix, Booking.com, Yandex, DoorDash, Snap, Uber, LinkedIn, and Meta — though no company publishes what share of its *own* experiments actually use it.

**As in Section 3.5, this subsection separates two different questions: interim monitoring (when accumulated outcome data is analyzed) and sequential allocation (when/how traffic-assignment itself runs).** EDI's `one-by-one`/`many-by-many` families describe the latter.

#### Interim monitoring (sequential testing)

No industry-wide percentage exists, but the qualitative picture is unusually clear: sequential or always-valid testing is now the default or a prominently offered method at essentially every major platform examined — Optimizely (since January 2015, applied to "hundreds of thousands of experiments" to date), Statsig (mSPRT-based, evaluated on 560 experiments and roughly 2,000 scorecard metrics), Eppo (explicitly the default), and in-house methodology built independently at Netflix and Spotify. Booking.com offers group-sequential designs. The severity of the alternative is well documented: even at 10,000 samples — "quite common in online A/B testing" — naive repeated peeking can inflate the false-positive rate 5–10× ([Johari, Koomen, Pekelis & Walsh 2017, KDD](http://library.usc.edu.ph/ACM/KKD%202017/pdfs/p1517.pdf)), and Netflix's own simulation of naive repeated testing found **70% of null-effect simulations eventually declared significance** ([Netflix TechBlog](https://netflixtechblog.com/sequential-a-b-testing-keeps-the-world-streaming-netflix-part-1-continuous-data-cba6c7ed49df)). Fixed-horizon testing has not been eliminated even at the platform that pioneered commercial always-valid inference: Optimizely still documents "Frequentist (Fixed Horizon)" as a distinct supported option, with its own peeking-ban warning still published in current docs.

Among companies that monitor sequentially, the cadence question — continuous versus scheduled-batch looks — has the cleanest concrete evidence in this report, and it is again a monitoring-cadence finding, not an allocation-timing one, despite sharing the same one-at-a-time-versus-batched shape as EDI's allocation families below. **Spotify explicitly selected group-sequential testing over continuous options (mSPRT, GAVI) because its own data pipeline runs in daily batches** — "most data jobs are run daily, implying one analysis per day during an experiment" — and its own simulation study spanned **14, 28, 42, or 56 evenly spaced looks** over experiments running a few weeks at most, roughly one look per day ([Spotify Engineering 2023](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2023/03/choosing-sequential-testing-framework-comparisons-and-discussions)). Booking.com names an exact design: **5 interim analyses, each after a batch of 200 new visitors** ([Booking.com/booking.ai](https://booking.ai/sequential-testing-at-booking-com-650954a569c7)). By contrast, Netflix and Eppo frame their statistical guarantees as genuinely continuous — valid after every single new observation, checkable at any time. Statsig sits in between: its underlying mSPRT statistic is mathematically continuous, but its dashboards refresh on an hourly or daily schedule by default, and its sequential test requires pre-setting a target duration up front — a scheduling step layered on a continuous guarantee. **A statistical guarantee that is continuous by construction, operated in practice on a batched cadence because the underlying data pipeline is itself batched, is a genuinely distinct phenomenon from clinical trials' history (Section 3.5), where per-subject monitoring was abandoned as impractical decades ago.** The best-evidence estimate for typical interim-look count is **5 to 56**, with a daily cadence over 2–8 weeks (≈14–56 looks) the best-attested modal pattern.

#### Sequential allocation

This is the question EDI's `fixed` / `one-by-one` / `many-by-many` families actually answer for online experiments: how and when the traffic-assignment mechanism itself runs, not when the resulting outcome data is analyzed.

**Fixed allocation is the stated default, and this is now directly confirmed at the mechanism level, not just asserted.** Simple per-user (hash-based Bernoulli) randomization is explicitly named as the default mechanism ([Kohavi & Longbotham 2023](https://exp-platform.com/Documents/2023-03-11EncyclopeiaMLDSABTestingFinal.pdf)). LinkedIn's own engineering blog settles exactly how: "variant assignment of a single member is deterministic (i.e., repeatable)," computed by a fixed hash formula — `HASH(salt)(member_id)` — applied identically to every member regardless of when they arrive, with no dependence on accumulated data or arrival time, chosen specifically because a deterministic rule requires no backend state or database lookup ([Ivaniuk & Duan 2020, LinkedIn Engineering](https://www.linkedin.com/blog/engineering/ab-testing-experimentation/a-b-testing-variant-assignment)). This is `fixed` allocation under EDI's typology despite the superficial appearance of sequential arrival.

**Switchback allocation is confirmed, in the allocating company's own words, as genuine `many-by-many` allocation** — not merely analogous to it. DoorDash's engineering blog states the mechanism directly: "we randomize the variant used for each time window, rather than simply randomizing the initial variant and flipping back and forth deterministically," with each time-region combination treated as its own randomized unit ([Kastelman & Ramesh 2018, DoorDash Engineering](https://careersatdoordash.com/blog/switchback-tests-and-randomized-experimentation-under-network-effects-at-doordash/)). Typical parameters are 30-minute windows at roughly city-level geography. DoorDash's own characterization of its prevalence is informative: "although the majority of the experiments conducted at DoorDash are A/B tests or difference-in-difference analyses, DoorDash occasionally relies on" switchback testing ([Sneider & Tang 2019, DoorDash Engineering](https://careersatdoordash.com/blog/experiment-rigor-for-switchback-experiment-analysis/)) — switchback, though a clean real-world instance of `many-by-many` allocation, is explicitly a minority practice even at the marketplace companies that pioneered it. Switchback has been in use at Lyft since at least 2016 ([Chamandy 2016, Lyft Engineering](https://eng.lyft.com/experimentation-in-a-ridesharing-marketplace-b39db027a66e)) and is named as a standard supported methodology on Uber's experimentation platform, alongside multi-armed bandits ([Uber Engineering 2018](https://www.uber.com/en-CA/blog/xp/)). A frequently repeated "~160-minute switchback window" figure attributed to Uber could not be traced to any Uber primary source and appears to originate from a third-party case-study or interview-prep site rather than Uber's own engineering documentation — it should not be cited as Uber-confirmed.

At least one major company explicitly recommends moving *away* from stratified randomization at assignment, toward post-hoc stratification and CUPED instead ([Xie & Aurisset 2016, KDD, Netflix](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2939672.2939733)) — the opposite trajectory from clinical trials, where stratification at randomization remains standard. Block randomization by name, and covariate-adaptive assignment as opposed to covariate *adjustment*, were not evidenced as deployed practice at any company examined in this sweep — a further gap, not a measured absence.

**Bandit and Thompson-sampling-based allocation — EDI's `one-by-one` response-adaptive family for online experiments — is technically mature, commercially available, and real at scale, but a minority practice relative to fixed-ratio allocation, and no industry-wide adoption percentage exists anywhere.** That absence was checked directly, including at vendors with every commercial incentive to publish a flattering number if one existed — Optimizely offers three distinct adaptive-allocation products (a UCB-style Stats Accelerator, Thompson-sampling multi-armed bandits for binary metrics, and contextual bandits) and VWO offers Thompson-sampling bandits, but neither publishes usage statistics ([Optimizely Support](https://support.optimizely.com/hc/en-us/articles/4410289050893-Experimentation-distribution-methods); [VWO Blog](https://vwo.com/blog/multi-armed-bandit-algorithm/)). Real production deployments exist and work: Microsoft's Decision Service, a contextual-bandit system built for exactly this purpose, reported 25–30% click-through improvements on content-recommendation deployments and an 18% revenue lift on a landing-page deployment ([Agarwal et al. 2016, Microsoft Research](https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.03966)); the foundational contextual-bandit paper, evaluated on over 33 million Yahoo events, reported a 12.5% click lift over a context-free bandit baseline ([Li, Chu, Langford & Schapire 2010, WWW](https://arxiv.org/abs/1003.0146v2)); Uber's platform names multi-armed bandits as one of several first-class supported methodologies. But the qualitative consensus is that this remains niche relative to fixed-ratio testing: "companies rarely boast about using multi-armed bandit models," and in mainstream industry practice "everything is either a t-test or linear regression" ([Au 2024, Counting Stuff](https://www.counting-stuff.com/whatever-happened-to-the-multi-armed-bandit/)) — bandits occupy a small niche despite roughly fifteen years of demonstrated production capability.

**Staged/ramped rollout ("canarying") is best classified as a distinct practice, not as a fourth instance of sequential allocation.** Despite surface resemblance to `many-by-many` allocation — each stage exposes a new group — it lacks the defining feature of switchback: a persisting, re-randomized control arm compared throughout. Google's own SRE literature defines canarying as "a partial and time-limited deployment of a change in a service and its evaluation," framed explicitly around defect detection ("gain confidence that it has no ill effects"), not treatment-effect estimation ([Google SRE Workbook](https://sre.google/workbook/canarying-releases/)). Meta's description of its staged-rollout mechanism ("Gatekeeper") contains no randomization operator at all — percentage gates (2% of production, then 100%) are administrative, and the practice converges to a single arm with no control left at the end state, rather than an alternating comparison ([Meta Engineering 2017](https://engineering.fb.com/2017/08/31/web/rapid-release-at-massive-scale/)). Staged rollout borrows A/B-testing vocabulary — "canary" versus "control" — but is conceptually and operationally a deployment risk-management practice, not a randomized experimental design, and should not be conflated with EDI's `many-by-many` allocation family.

## 5. Economics and political science

### 5.1 The audit does not exist

**No study in either discipline codes the statistical family of primary outcomes.** The meta-research literature in economics and political science audits *design* (field/lab/survey, factorial), *multiplicity* (number of primary outcomes and hypotheses), *estimator* (OLS versus MLE), and *transformations* (log, inverse hyperbolic sine) — but never the distribution type of the outcome. One reason is visible in the registries themselves: across 300 randomly drawn AEA registrations, the restrictiveness of outcome descriptions averaged **2 on a 0–5 scale** ("number of fruits consumed"), and no outcome description reached 5 ([Abrams, Libgober & List 2020, NBER WP 27250](https://www.nber.org/papers/w27250)).

Everything in Section 5.3 is therefore **triangulated from estimator shares and canonical outcomes, not counted**. The honest framing for EDI is that nobody has run this audit.

### 5.2 What is solidly established

**Multiple outcomes are the norm, and they are the single most robust finding in this literature.** The average preregistration specifies **3 primary outcomes** ([Abrams, Libgober & List 2020](https://www.nber.org/papers/w27250)); across 898 field RCTs registered 2013–2016 the average is **6**, of which only half ever appear in the paper's abstract, and the median trial in that registry enrolls **2,000 observations** ([Leight, Asri & Imai 2025](https://taisukeimai.com/api/resources/rct_registry.pdf)). Among 195 pre-analysis plans, **47% specify more than 10 hypotheses and 8% specify more than 50**, yet only **29% of plans with more than 5 hypotheses pre-commit to any multiple-testing correction** ([Ofosu & Posner 2021, Perspectives on Politics](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/perspectives-on-politics/article/preanalysis-plans-an-early-stocktaking/94E7FAE76001C45A04E8F5E272C773CE)). The standard response is index aggregation: the six-country graduation program pooled **21,063 adults** into **10 z-score indices** with q-values, measured at two follow-up rounds ([Banerjee et al. 2015, Science](https://villageenterprise.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/12/Science-2015-Banerjee-1.pdf)).

**Factorial/multi-treatment designs run at about a fifth of field experiments:** **27 of 124** top-five field experiments 2007–2017 ([Muralidharan, Romero & Wüthrich 2025, REStat](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w26562/w26562.pdf)).

**Money outcomes carry large masses at zero.** Verified zero shares: **24%** of MEPS person-years have zero healthcare spending and **30.8%** (137/445) of the canonical NSW sample had zero 1978 earnings ([Mullahy & Norton 2024, OBES](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w30735/w30735.pdf)); across seven microcredit RCTs, the spike at zero accounts for **up to 50%** of the profit, revenue and expenditure distributions, which invalidates classical quantile asymptotics ([Meager 2022, AER](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/115559/1/Meager_Distributional_Effects_Aggregation_August_2021.pdf)). The methodological workaround is widespread enough that **17 AER papers published since 2018** applied an arcsinh transform to such outcomes, 10 of them interpreting the result as a percentage change — and in 5 of the 10 replicable cases, merely rescaling the outcome by 100 moved the estimate by more than 100% ([Chen & Roth 2024, QJE](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.06080)).

**Ordinal outcomes are almost never modelled as ordinal.** Across 73 reproduced subjective-wellbeing papers in top-30 economics journals (1,610 regressions, 28,522 coefficients), **fewer than 2% used ordered probit**, 3% used probit-adjusted OLS, and about 2% dichotomized — roughly **93–95% linear**. The consequences are not cosmetic: about 25% of published results sign-reverse under a plausible scale transformation, and about 60% under a purely ordinal interpretation ([Kaiser & Lepinteur 2026, arXiv 2507.16440](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.16440)). This is latent rather than revealed demand for a proper `ordinal` type — practitioners default to OLS because that is what the toolchain makes easy.

**Bounded proportions dominate laboratory games.** The dictator-game meta-study covers **129 contributions, 616 treatments, 20,813 observations**, with a mean gift of **28.35%** of the pie and point masses at the boundaries: **36.11% give nothing, 16.74% choose the equal split, 5.44% give everything** ([Engel 2011, Exp Econ](http://homepage.coll.mpg.de/pdf_dat/2010_07online.pdf)). That is a bounded [0,1] outcome with atoms at 0, 0.5 and 1 — not plain `continuous`, and not fully served by `proportion` either.

**Political science leans binary, and collapses nominal outcomes into binary.** Turnout field experiments use validated binary turnout from voter files — **85 direct-mail experiments** in one meta-analysis ([Gerber & Green 2016](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/sites/default/files/research-paper/Gerber%20Green%20Handbook.pdf)). The 49-experiment persuasion meta-analysis explicitly recodes multi-candidate vote choice to 0/1 "so that the estimates always have the interpretation of percentage point effect on vote share," and analyses precinct-level designs as vote shares ([Kalla & Broockman 2018, APSR](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/103775sx)). Nominal outcomes exist in this field and are still analysed as binaries.

**Choice and rating outcomes are large and growing.** **124 published conjoint articles** cite the 2014 methods paper, 27% of them on voting and candidate choice, with rating and forced choice named as the two most common outcome measures ([Bansak et al., Cambridge Handbook](https://cpb-us-w2.wpmucdn.com/web.sas.upenn.edu/dist/f/49/files/2019/09/handbook-draft-07-09202019-1.pdf)); an independent ten-journal review found **59 conjoint articles in 2014–2019** ([de la Cuesta, Egami & Imai 2022, Political Analysis](https://imai.fas.harvard.edu/research/files/conjoint.pdf)); one meta-analysis standardizes **67 candidate-choice experiments** ([Schwarz & Coppock 2022, JOP](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/716290)).

**Repeated measures are rarer here than in medicine, at least in survey experiments.** Coding 67 political-science survey-experiment studies: **post-only 82%, pre-post 7%, within-subjects 1%, conjoint 6%** — only about **18%** depart from a simple post-only design ([Clifford, Sheagley & Piston 2021, APSR](http://scottaclifford.com/wp-content/uploads/2012/02/CSP_repeated-measures.pdf)). Economics field trials differ: multiple follow-up rounds are routine in flagship trials.

The strongest single anchor is the estimator split in 53 experimental papers with 1,780 regressions: **67% OLS, 22% MLE** ([Young 2019, QJE](https://personal.lse.ac.uk/YoungA/ChannellingFisherQJE.pdf)). The MLE share is a *floor* on non-continuous outcomes, since binary outcomes are routinely estimated by linear probability models.

A final caution on commensurability: even within a single intervention family in the AidGrade database of roughly **600 papers across 20 intervention types**, fewer than half of studies share a narrowly comparable outcome definition ([Vivalt, World Bank Development Impact](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/impactevaluations/what-do-600-papers-20-types-interventions-tell-us-about-how-much-impact-evaluations-generalize-guest)).

### 5.4 Sample size, covariate adjustment, and design timing in economics and political science

This subsection draws on the same follow-up sweep as Sections 3.5 and 6.8, for the same four questions. It also corrects a real citation trap worth flagging on its own: the paper most search engines return for "Bruhn & McKenzie 2009" at a common URL is actually a different, similarly-titled paper (Guiteras, Levine & Polley 2015). The genuine Bruhn & McKenzie 2009 was located and verified separately, in full.

**Sample size, and a real historical trend.** The median field RCT in the AEA registry (2013–2016, n=898) enrolls **2,000 observations**, with roughly half using a cluster or multilevel design ([Leight, Asri & Imai 2025](https://www.jessicaleight.com/uploads/1/3/2/3/13234647/rct_registry_merged.pdf)). This is roughly an order of magnitude larger than field RCTs a decade earlier: a 2008 literature review of 18 published development experiments found **typical sample sizes of 100–300 units**, or "100 to 500 individuals, or 20 to 100 schools or health clinics" ([Bruhn & McKenzie 2009, AEJ:Applied](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/372631468177851332/pdf/WPS4752.pdf)) — a genuine secular increase in typical field-experiment scale, not sampling noise. Lab economics runs by a different convention: **at least 30 subjects per treatment cell** is the durable norm, so total N scales with the number of arms rather than following a fixed target ([List, Sadoff & Wagner 2011, Exp Econ](https://rady.ucsd.edu/_files/faculty-research/sadoff/List_Sadoff_Wagner_Optimal_Sample_Design_Experimental_Economics_2011.pdf)). Among pre-registered social-science experiments, field designs dominate — **63–89%** across two independent registry samples — with survey experiments at **27%** and lab experiments a consistent small minority at **4–11%** ([Ofosu & Posner 2021, Perspectives on Politics](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/112747/1/pre_analysis_plans_an_early_stocktaking.pdf); Leight, Asri & Imai 2025). Development economics specifically is somewhat underpowered relative to its own expectations: a median power of **59%** to detect the researchers' own predicted effect, with only about a third of studies exceeding the conventional 80% threshold (Coville & Vivalt 2017, as quoted directly in [Czibor, Jimenez-Gomez & List 2019, NBER WP 25451](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w25451/w25451.pdf) — the original was not independently accessed, so this figure is one level removed from the primary source).

**Covariate adjustment.** Bruhn & McKenzie's literature review of 18 development experiments found **13 (72%) used stratification**, 2 (11%) matched pairs, and 3 (17%) pure randomization; their own survey of 25 leading development-RCT researchers, reporting on their most recent experiment (23 respondents), found a similar split — **stratification ≈61%, matched pairs ≈35%, pure randomization ≈4%**. Among the 14 stratified experiments in that survey, the number of stratification variables was small: **43% used one variable** (typically geographic location), 29% used two, and 29% used four ([Bruhn & McKenzie 2009](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/372631468177851332/pdf/WPS4752.pdf)). A separate, broader count — variables used just to *check* balance after the fact, not to stratify — ran wider, 4 to 39 across the reviewed papers, "typically 5 to 20." As in the clinical and education literatures, pre-registration does not translate into disclosed practice: **44% of 195 pre-analysis plans left the number of pre-specified control variables unclear**, and in 19% of cases the published model deviated from the registered one ([Ofosu & Posner 2021](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/112747/1/pre_analysis_plans_an_early_stocktaking.pdf)). No methodological guide reviewed — including Duflo, Glennerster & Kremer's own toolkit and EGAP's covariate-adjustment guide — gives a numeric target; both offer only qualitative advice ("stratify on everything available" versus "adjust only for outcome-predictive covariates").

**Sequential allocation.** Unlike the clinical-trials and online-experimentation literatures, economics has little to no separate literature on sequential *testing*/interim monitoring of outcome data as a distinct topic — pre-analysis plans specify a single analysis at a pre-set sample size, and the material below is genuinely about the allocation mechanism itself, not about monitoring cadence.

Adaptive and sequential *allocation* is close to absent from economics, and the evidence for this is unusually direct. The paper that introduced the leading adaptive-assignment method for the field — a response-adaptive design in EDI's terms, since treatment probabilities shift with accumulating outcome data — states plainly that such designs "are not yet common in economics" ([Kasy & Sautmann 2021, Econometrica](https://maxkasy.github.io/home/files/papers/adaptiveexperimentspolicy.pdf)) — a claim from the flagship source for the method, not an outside critic. A 2015 methods paper studying the formally optimal sequential-allocation method found **zero known field implementations of it across clinical trials or economics**, confirmed against three clinical-trials survey articles and personal communication with one of their authors, and could name only **two known instances of even simple sequential randomization in the entire economics literature** ([Guiteras, Levine & Polley 2015, CEGA WPS-050](https://escholarship.org/content/qt74g0h3f8/qt74g0h3f8_noSplash_ce166394b0e86fded17321c880307bba.pdf)). The 898-trial AEA registry sample shows no adaptive-design category anywhere in its summary statistics — consistent with, though not a direct count of, negligible prevalence.

**One-by-one versus many-by-many allocation.** Unlike psychology and education, this design family is explicitly named and methodologically endorsed in development economics, under the label "randomized order of phase-in" ([Duflo, Glennerster & Kremer 2006, NBER TWP 333](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/t0333/t0333.pdf)). Its canonical worked example is the Miguel & Kremer (2004) deworming study: 75 Kenyan primary schools split into three groups of 25, phased in over 1998, 1999, and 2000 — a textbook three-wave, 25-schools-per-wave `many-by-many` design. A 2015 methods paper independently describes the same structure almost word for word, for villages enrolled sequentially into a water-filter demand study, explicitly distinguishing it from pure one-by-one sequential allocation because "the researcher knows the values of the covariates for all households in village v, and can assign treatments simultaneously within village v" ([Guiteras, Levine & Polley 2015](https://escholarship.org/content/qt74g0h3f8/qt74g0h3f8_noSplash_ce166394b0e86fded17321c880307bba.pdf)) — a near-exact match to EDI's own `many-by-many` definition. This connects to the broader staggered-rollout and stepped-wedge literature ([Xiong, Athey, Bayati & Imbens](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1911.03764)). Roughly **50% of modern AEA-registered field RCTs use a cluster or multilevel design** ([Leight, Asri & Imai 2025](https://www.jessicaleight.com/uploads/1/3/2/3/13234647/rct_registry_merged.pdf)), which is structurally where phased enrollment arises — but no source quantifies what share of trials phase enrollment versus randomize everyone at once, a genuine gap in the methodological literature rather than a search failure.

**Randomization method (allocation).** Bruhn & McKenzie's practitioner survey gives the richest breakdown in this report for any field: among 25 researchers, **80% had ever used simple randomization** (rising to 94% among those with 5+ trials of experience), **56% had ever used pair-wise matching** (39% in their most recent experiment), and re-randomization — redrawing when a balance check looks poor — had been used by **32%** (subjective imbalance judgment) to **46%** (among experienced researchers) despite the paper's own simulations concluding matching is generally preferable. A "multiple-draws" variant (100–1,000 draws, keeping the best-balanced) had been used by 24–38%. The number of strata used, when stratification was used, ranged 3–200 (median 18), with a median of 36 treated observations per stratum. Randomization was conducted privately in 74% of cases, publicly in 13%, and semi-publicly in 13% ([Bruhn & McKenzie 2009](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/372631468177851332/pdf/WPS4752.pdf)).

## 6. Psychology, sociology, education, criminology, and behavioral trials

As in economics, almost nobody audits outcome *data type* directly here; most shares below are inferred from what the audits do measure (standardized mean differences versus odds ratios, LMM versus GLMM, endpoint definitions). Three exceptions carry unusual weight.

### 6.1 Education: the cleanest field-level result in the review

Across **141 large EEF and NCEE K-12 achievement RCTs** covering 1,222,024 students (**median 2,386 per trial**; the mean, ≈8,668, is far higher because the distribution is heavily right-skewed), **every single trial reported its outcomes as standardized mean differences** — a 141/141 continuous corpus ([Lortie-Forgues & Inglis 2019, Educational Researcher](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/141754/3/LFI_2019_manuscript_supp.pdf)). The trials produced 271 distinct effect sizes, a derived **1.92 outcomes per trial**.

That 100% is partly definitional: eligibility required academic outcomes and excluded qualitative pilots. Attendance, exclusion and behavior trials exist and would push binary, proportion and count upward, so ~90–95% is the ceiling for the achievement subset and perhaps 75–85% for education RCTs generally. The dominant non-independence in education is hierarchical clustering by class and school, not repeated measurement within subject.

### 6.2 Clinical psychology: binary endpoints are usually derived, not measured

Across **228 randomized trials** of psychotherapy for depression, response rates were actually *reported* in **25 trials (11.0%)** and **imputed from means and standard deviations in the other 203 (89.0%)** ([Cuijpers et al. 2021, Acta Psychiatr Scand](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8457213/)). The primary outcome is a continuous symptom scale in roughly nine trials out of ten; the binary response/remission figure that gets quoted downstream is a post-hoc dichotomization.

This field also supplies a clean censored-continuous case: remission is defined as scoring **≤7 on the HAM-D**, so the scale bottoms out exactly where treatment succeeds.

### 6.3 Experimental psychology: repeated measures are the default, and half the outcomes are non-Gaussian

Across **400 papers** using mixed-effects models, the model split was LMM 193, GLMM 88, both 95, GAM 5 — so a derived **45.8% fit a GLMM at some point**, meaning a non-Gaussian outcome such as accuracy or counts. **277 of 400 (69.3%)** included crossed random intercepts for both participants *and* items ([Meteyard & Davies 2020, J Mem Lang](https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/140728/1/DaviesMeteyard_MixedModels_Revised2_13012020.pdf)).

A discipline-wide count puts *detected* repeated-measures analysis lower, falling from **25% to 16%** across 57,909 articles in 2010–2021 ([PLOS ONE 2023](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0283353)) — but that denominator includes correlational work, so it is a floor rather than a contradiction.

Note a representational subtlety that matters for EDI: trial-level accuracy (binary) and subject-level percent-correct (proportion) are the *same data at two aggregation levels*. EDI's `incidence` and `proportion` types will frequently be competing encodings of one dataset rather than markers of different studies.

### 6.4 Social psychology: ordinal data, metric models, every time

The load-bearing finding: surveying all articles in JPSP, Psychological Science, and JEP:General that mention "Likert", **100% of the articles that analyzed ordinal data did so using a metric model** ([Liddell & Kruschke 2018, JESP](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2018.08.009)). The denominator behind that percentage could not be verified, but the 100% itself is verbatim from the abstract.

The case for EDI's `ordinal` type is therefore **not that ordinal outcomes are frequent — it is that they are universally mishandled.** That matches the economics finding independently (~93–95% linear models on ordinal wellbeing outcomes), from a completely different literature.

### 6.5 Addiction and behavioral trials: the best evidence anywhere for `proportion`

This is the only corpus that directly enumerates endpoint types. Across **118 alcohol use disorder pharmacotherapy trials** ([Belnap et al. 2024, Pharmaceut Med](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11272707/)):

| Endpoint | Trials | Share | EDI type |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Percent days abstinent | 78 | 66.1% | `proportion` |
| Return to any drinking | 66 | 55.9% | `incidence` (natively `survival`) |
| Return to heavy drinking | 51 | 43.2% | `incidence` (natively `survival`) |
| Percent heavy drinking days | 48 | 40.7% | `proportion` |
| Drinks per drinking day | 35 | 29.7% | `count` / semi-continuous |
| Drinks per day | 17 | 14.4% | `count` / semi-continuous |
| Both abstinence and heavy-drinking endpoints | 74 | 62.7% | multivariate |

**A bounded [0,1] proportion is the single most common endpoint type in this literature.** That is striking against Section 3, where the entire clinical audit literature offered exactly one review with a "percentage" column at 2 of 100. The shares sum well past 100% because trials routinely carry three to five endpoints of different types at once.

Multiplicity is extreme and measured: across **405 alcohol brief-intervention trials, 2,641 separate outcomes were measured in approximately 1,560 different ways**, a derived **6.5 outcomes per trial**, and **no single outcome appeared in all trials** ([J Stud Alcohol Drugs 2019](https://doi.org/10.15288/jsad.2019.80.286)).

### 6.6 Criminology and sociology: time-to-event data, analysed as binary

A meta-analysis of **29 prison-based RCTs** (9,443 participants, a derived **≈326 per trial**) ran entirely on binary recidivism — reconviction, reincarceration, rearrest, parole violation or new charges — and where a study had multiple assessments, "the most serious outcome" was chosen for pooling ([Beaudry et al. 2021, Lancet Psychiatry](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8376657/)). Follow-up periods varied without limit. Recidivism is natively time-to-event with variable censoring, and is also natively recurrent and zero-inflated; it is analysed as a single binary.

Sociology's signature experimental form is the audit study, which is binary by construction. The canonical meta-analysis covers **28 studies, 55,842 applications for 26,326 positions** (a derived **≈940 positions per study**) with callback as the outcome ([Quillian et al. 2017, PNAS](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5642692/)). The derived **2.12 applications per position** means the standard structure is *paired/clustered binary within job posting*, not independent draws.

Volume context matters when weighting these fields: since 2010, field experiments were **4.4% of articles in top political science journals and 7.8% in top economics journals, but under 1% in sociology** ([Baldassarri & Abascal 2017, Annu Rev Sociol](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-soc-073014-112445)).

### 6.8 Sample size, covariate adjustment, and design timing in psychology, education, and criminology

This subsection draws on the same follow-up sweep as Section 3.5, for the same four questions, in this field cluster. Coverage is uneven: some figures are well quantified, and two structural findings — the informal-versus-formal nature of "sequential" behavior, and cohort-based randomization — are genuine gaps in how the methods literature conceptualizes this field, not gaps in this search.

**Sample size.** Cognitive and social-personality psychology run far smaller than clinical trials. Across four APA journals sampled at 1955, 1977, 1995, and 2006, median **N per condition ran 10–26** and was essentially flat over the 1977–2006 window despite decades of calls for larger samples ([Marszalek et al. 2011, Perceptual and Motor Skills](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.2466/03.11.PMS.112.2.331-348)); median total N per study over the same span ran 32–60. Social-personality psychology specifically averages higher: a mean total N of **104** across 1,934 studies (2006–2010), with power to detect a typical effect size around only 50%, and per-journal five-year medians ranging 73–178 ([Fraley & Vazire 2014, PLOS ONE](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4189949/)).

Education RCTs corroborate the median already used elsewhere in this report: **2,386 per trial** across 141 EEF/NCEE trials ([Lortie-Forgues & Inglis 2019](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/141754/3/LFI_2019_manuscript_supp.pdf)), typically spanning dozens of schools — a companion IES review of 37 cluster RCTs found scales up to 449 districts, 1,479 schools, and 971,263 students combined ([Tipton et al. 2021, Educational Researcher](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1291329.pdf)). Criminology has no clean verified median N, but a well-corroborated qualitative finding instead: **"Weisburd's paradox"**, where effect size and statistical power are empirically almost uncorrelated with sample size across criminology experiments, independently reproduced against a dataset of 66 studies and 402 effect estimates ([Nelson, Wooditch & Dario 2015](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11292-014-9214-y); [Gelman & Carlin 2017](https://sites.stat.columbia.edu/gelman/research/published/weisburd_28.05.2017.pdf)). Sociology audit studies run at a derived **≈1,995 applications per study** (55,842 applications / 28 studies — the randomized unit; the ≈940 positions/study figure already in Table 8.7 is the clustering unit, since roughly 2.12 applications go to each position) ([Quillian et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5642692/)).

**Covariate adjustment.** The cleanest, most authoritative statement here is a funder's own written policy rather than an audit: the UK's Education Endowment Foundation mandates a default model with **exactly one substantive covariate — the pre-test/baseline score** — plus treatment status and any stratification variable, explicitly to avoid "the fishing problem," citing [Simmons, Nelson & Simonsohn 2011](https://www2.psych.ubc.ca/~schaller/528Readings/SimmonsNelsonSimonsohn2011.pdf) by name ([EEF Statistical Analysis Guidance 2022](https://d2tic4wvo1iusb.cloudfront.net/documents/evaluation/evaluation-design/EEF-Analysis-Guidance-Website-Version-2022.14.11.pdf)). No dedicated census of covariate counts in psychology experiments specifically was located — a genuine literature gap, not a search failure — so the nearest quantified benchmark is cross-disciplinary: **75% of trials that use any covariate at randomization use only one or two** ([Ciolino et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6610785/), already cited in Section 3.5 for clinical trials).

**Interim monitoring (sequential testing) — and a genuinely different phenomenon than clinical monitoring.** This paragraph is about when outcome data is analyzed, not about allocation; the allocation-timing findings for this field cluster follow in the next two paragraphs. No formal, pre-registered sequential design is standard practice in any of these fields; fixed-N designs dominate by construction, and none of the trial reviews surveyed describe a stopping rule as a design feature. What exists instead is **informal, undisclosed optional stopping** — a questionable research practice, not a designed method. Surveying 2,155 academic psychologists, **55.9–58.0% self-admitted deciding whether to collect more data after checking for significance**, and **15.6–22.5% self-admitted stopping early specifically because a desired result had appeared** ([John, Loewenstein & Prelec 2012, Psychological Science](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/loewenstein/MeasPrevalQuestTruthTelling.pdf)) — both likely underestimates by the authors' own argument. Simulating exactly this behavior inflates the false-positive rate from a nominal 5% to **7.7%** (a derived +54% relative increase), and combining optional stopping with three other common undisclosed choices raises it to **60.7%** ([Simmons, Nelson & Simonsohn 2011](https://www2.psych.ubc.ca/~schaller/528Readings/SimmonsNelsonSimonsohn2011.pdf)). Principled, pre-registered Bayesian sequential designs exist and have grown since roughly 2015, but **no quantitative adoption-rate estimate exists in the literature** — report this qualitatively only. **The contrast with Section 3.5 is stark: clinical trials that monitor sequentially do so formally, through a committee, with pre-specified boundaries; psychology's equivalent behavior is almost entirely informal and undisclosed.**

**Sequential allocation: one-by-one versus many-by-many.** No quantified split exists, but the structural evidence points toward `many-by-many` being the norm without the methods literature naming it as such. The EEF's own guidance explicitly flags "multiple cohorts using different pre-tests" as a standard, expected feature of its funded trials requiring specific statistical handling — direct confirmation that large UK education RCTs are routinely multi-cohort, recruiting schools in waves across multiple years. No education- or criminology-specific methodological paper was found that names and formally analyzes cohort-based randomization as its own design category, in contrast to the adjacent but distinct "trials within cohorts" literature in health services research (Relton et al. 2010), which describes a standing observational cohort sampled repeatedly rather than sequential batch randomization of arriving cohorts. **This is a genuine conceptual gap rather than an evidence gap** — the practice matches EDI's `many-by-many` family closely, but nobody in this literature has framed it that way.

**Randomization method (allocation).** The strongest quantified source in this field cluster: across **64 UK school-based cluster RCTs** for health outcomes, randomization was **school-level in 88%, class-level in 9%, year-group in 3%**, and the balancing method was **stratified in 45%, matched in 13%, minimisation in 13%, constrained in 9%, and simple/complete in 20%** — a clear majority (80%) used some restricted method ([Parker et al. 2021, BMC Med Res Methodol](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8311976/)). This is health-outcome trials specifically rather than academic-achievement trials, but it is UK-representative and the best-quantified figure available for education. Criminology has a qualitative rather than quantified consensus: matched-pair and blocked designs are a distinctive, historically established tradition, illustrated by Weisburd's Redlands, California policing experiment (26 census blocks grouped into 13 matched pairs on risk score, calls-for-service, and population density), with a dedicated historical review tracing matched-pair adoption in criminology and medicine from 1926–2021 ([Braga et al. 2022, J Exp Criminol](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11292-021-09504-y); [Weisburd & Gill 2014](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10940-013-9195-4)). No equivalent randomization-method census exists for psychology experiments — a further gap consistent with these being typically small, single-site studies randomized by convention rather than by any documented, audited standard.

## 7. Structural features layered on existing types

These are the properties that co-occur with a measurement scale, and they map onto most of EDI's planned work.

| Feature | Prevalence | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Repeated measures collected** | **79%** of top-journal RCTs (61/77) | [Bell et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4247714/) |
| **Repeated measures analysed as such** | **18%** (14/77); mixed models 15%, GEE 4% | same |

Three observations follow.

**The repeated-measures gap is an analysis gap, not a data gap.** Four in five trials collect longitudinal outcomes and one in five analyse them that way. EDI's planned `longitudinal` type addresses a structure that is already almost universally present in the data.

**Competing risks are the most mishandled structure in the trial literature.** 77.5% of 40 top-journal survival RCTs were susceptible, 77.4% of those still reported Kaplan-Meier, and only 16.1% used cumulative incidence functions ([Austin & Fine 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28102550/)). In geriatric oncology the figure is starker: **1 of 41 trials** used a competing-risks analysis ([Burdett et al. 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30011032/)). The value of implementing this correctly exceeds what the raw usage share suggests.

**Compositional and rank/choice outcomes are genuinely rare in randomized experiments.** Zero RCTs appeared across the design-agnostic compositional reviews, and DOOR endpoints number twelve trials worldwide. Both are planned for v2.0.0; the evidence supports treating them as low-priority within that release.

**Four findings cut across these tables.** First, wherever a funder or platform states a default covariate policy, it converges on exactly **one** covariate — the pre-period or baseline value of the outcome itself — in clinical trials, online experimentation, and education alike, each independently justified by diminishing returns or a documented "researcher degrees of freedom" concern. Second, on interim monitoring (Table E), scheduled-batch cadences dominate wherever sequential testing is used at all, for field-specific reasons: clinical trials because per-subject monitoring was judged operationally impractical as early as the 1970s, online platforms because the underlying data pipeline is itself batched. Third, on allocation (Table F), `fixed` allocation is the dominant real-world mechanism in both clinical trials and online experimentation; `many-by-many` allocation is explicitly named and evidenced in economics (phased cluster rollout) and structurally implied in education, while genuinely adaptive `one-by-one` allocation — minimization, response-adaptive randomization, bandits — remains a real but consistently small minority everywhere it has been measured so far. Fourth, sample size spans roughly **five orders of magnitude** across Table D, from ~10 per condition in cognitive psychology to several million per experiment in large-scale online platforms — the single largest source of variation in this entire report, and the strongest argument for why a package like EDI needs both exact/permutation-based inference (valid at every scale) and asymptotic shortcuts (efficient at the largest scales).

**Two mapping cautions matter more than any point estimate.** Bernoulli allocation uses independent assignments and leaves realized arm totals random; iBCRD fixes arm totals. Audits that combine “simple/complete” cannot distinguish them. Enrollment over time, interim analyses, and staggered treatment delivery do not by themselves resolve assignment timing.

### 8.16 Table H — two-arm versus multi-arm experiments, all fields at a glance

Tables H–H4 mirror the overall and field-specific response-type and design-type summaries. **Two-arm means exactly two randomized treatment conditions in the experiment; multi-arm means three or more, including any control condition.** Two active treatments plus a control is three-arm. A between-subjects 2×2 factorial has four randomized cells even if each main effect is a binary contrast. Multiple outcomes, enrollment cohorts, countries, analysis subgroups, or pairwise comparisons do not create extra arms. Within-subjects conditions and conjoint attributes require separate coding rather than automatic conversion into parallel treatment arms.

This is a targeted arm-count follow-up to the existing audit, checked September 16, 2026. **No measured worldwide split or defensible working overall percentage was located.** Quantified samples below are retained separately rather than averaged across fields. ## 9. Implications for EDI

**The six implemented types are well chosen.** They cover roughly 99% of primary outcomes by measurement scale in every field surveyed. No evidence in this review suggests a missing scalar type that would materially expand coverage.

**`proportion` is the most under-appreciated type EDI already has.** The clinical audit literature has almost no column for it — one review at 2 of 100 — yet it is the *most common endpoint type* in alcohol trials (percent days abstinent, 66.1%), a core metric class in large-scale online experimentation (ratio metrics, 20–35%), the natural form of cognitive accuracy data, and the standard outcome in laboratory economics games. The category is invisible in audits because taxonomies fold it into "continuous", not because it is rare.

**The case for `ordinal` is mishandling, not frequency.** Two independent literatures converge: **100%** of surveyed social-psychology articles analysing ordinal data used a metric model, and **93–95%** of economics regressions on ordinal wellbeing outcomes are linear. In clinical trials, 31–60% of ordinal outcomes are dichotomized before analysis. The demand is latent, and the argument should be framed as correctness rather than prevalence.

**Multivariate support has the broadest evidence base of any planned type** — stronger than nominal, compositional, or rank, and measured directly in every field surveyed. It is currently scheduled for v2.0.0; the evidence would justify prioritising it within that release.

**Deferring `nominal` is well supported.** At ~0.1% of outcomes, with no review in any field counting it, and with practitioners collapsing unordered outcomes to one-vs-rest binaries even when they occur, the existing recommendation to defer holds.

**Competing risks and semi-continuous outcomes are the strongest v1.4.0 items on evidence.** Both are common and both are demonstrably mishandled today — Kaplan-Meier used on 77% of competing-risk-prone trials, and revenue metrics winsorized or binarized rather than modelled.

**Compositional and rank/choice should be justified on methodological grounds, not prevalence.** Zero RCTs appeared across the design-agnostic compositional reviews, and DOOR endpoints number twelve trials worldwide. Claiming measured demand for these would not survive scrutiny.

**The design-timing sweep (Tables 8.9–8.10) separates interim monitoring from sequential allocation, and each tells a different story for EDI.** On monitoring cadence (Table 8.9), scheduled-batch looks dominate everywhere sequential testing happens at all — clinical trials because per-subject monitoring was judged impractical as early as the 1970s, online platforms because their underlying data pipelines are themselves batched; continuous, subject-at-a-time monitoring has essentially no footprint in prospective experimentation outside a handful of online platforms' statistical guarantees and non-experimental drug-safety surveillance. On allocation (Table 8.10), `fixed` — a schedule generated before enrollment begins — is the dominant real-world mechanism in clinical trials and online experimentation alike; `many-by-many` allocation is explicitly named and evidenced in economics (phased cluster rollout, "randomized order of phase-in") and structurally implied in education, while genuinely adaptive `one-by-one` allocation (minimization, response-adaptive randomization, bandits) is a real but consistently small minority everywhere it has been measured — and a dedicated sweep on response-adaptive randomization and bandit allocation specifically was still in progress as this section was written. EDI's own `KK14`/`KK21` matching-on-the-fly algorithm — tracing to Kapelner & Krieger (2014) — has **no evidenced real-trial adoption of that specific algorithm**; it and its direct statistical descendants validate only through simulation or a retrospective reanalysis (synthetic outcomes on real covariate data from the REACH trial), not a trial designed and run with the method. That narrower claim should not be over-generalized, though: the broader design family — live, sequential, covariate-adaptive allocation — **is** validated in real, influential practice, just via related but distinct methods. The REACH trial's actual allocation was itself a live, `many-by-many` batch-matching procedure, and the ESCAPE stroke trial, a major practice-changing study, allocated its 316 participants using a genuinely `one-by-one`, real-time, server-based covariate-adaptive method (Minimal Sufficient Balance). The accurate framing for EDI's own algorithm is therefore "methodologically mature, with the general design family it belongs to proven in real trials, but the specific algorithm not yet independently adopted" — a more defensible and more interesting claim than "unadopted," and one worth preserving precisely in how these designs are documented.

**A defensible public statement:**

> EDI supports the six response types that account for the overwhelming majority of primary outcomes in randomized experiments — continuous, binary, proportion, count, time-to-event, and ordinal — across clinical trials, online experimentation, and the social sciences.

Avoid any claim of a precise worldwide response-type distribution. No such measurement exists.

## 10. Evidence gaps

Four of EDI's planned types have **no prevalence literature whatsoever**, and this is worth recording rather than papering over:

- **Nominal outcomes** — no methodological review counts them in RCTs. The nearest analogue, the ordinal scoping review, explicitly excludes unordered outcomes. The absence of even a review is itself evidence the category is small.
- **Recurrent events** — no review establishes a denominator. Only conditional method-use audits exist, such as 83 falls-prevention trials of which **fewer than one-third** used an appropriate recurrent-event method ([Donaldson et al. 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19106254/)).
- **Interval censoring** — no audit counts it; the 85.4% PFS figure above is a proxy resting on the fact that scheduled-imaging assessment produces interval-censored data ([Panageas et al. 2007, JNCI](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17374832/)), then analysed as right-censored anyway.
- **Network/interference outcomes** — no prevalence count exists in any literature searched.

Two further gaps constrain the whole report: no registry-wide typed classification of primary outcomes exists for any major registry, and bounded-fraction (`proportion`) outcomes have essentially no dedicated audit.

**A fifth gap, identified after the initial five-field sweep, has now been closed:** none of the sources fetched for response-type prevalence reported (a) the number of covariates adjusted for in the primary analysis model, (b) the prevalence of sequential/adaptive designs versus fixed-sample designs, (c) among sequential designs, the split between EDI's `one-by-one` (continuous, subject-at-a-time) and `many-by-many` (batched, pre-scheduled) timing families, or (d) the randomization-method taxonomy actually used (simple, block, stratified, minimization, matched-pair, cluster, rerandomization). A dedicated five-way follow-up sweep targeting exactly these four questions, mirroring the structure of the sweep behind Sections 3–6, is now complete and integrated: clinical trials in Section 3.5, online experimentation in Section 4.5, economics and political science in Section 5.4, psychology/education/criminology/sociology in Section 6.8, and a cross-cutting sweep on interim-monitoring mechanics and randomization-method taxonomy folded into Section 3.5. **A second, narrower follow-up sweep has since separated this material into interim monitoring versus sequential allocation specifically** (the two were conflated in an earlier draft), adding response-adaptive randomization, bandit-based allocation, stepped-wedge designs, and a corrected account of matching-on-the-fly's real-world adoption. That sweep is also complete and integrated. The cross-field comparison is in Tables 8.8–8.10.
